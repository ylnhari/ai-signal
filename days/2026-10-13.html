Meta launches Muse Wonder, a frontier model for personal and professional agents, via API and Muse Code

Meta announced Muse Wonder on 12 October, a new frontier model series aimed at personal and professional AI agents. Muse Wonder is available now through the Meta Model API and Muse Code, and Muse Wonder Full is available to Muse Code subscribers; consumer Muse experiences follow, subscribers first. Meta's pitch is long-running agent work: persistent memory across conversations, persistence on hard goals, domain expertise in law, finance and health, and long-horizon coding. It claims state-of-the-art results on agent, memory and coding benchmarks (LAB, FocusBench, SWE-Marathon), but the post gives no score table in text and no pricing, and a full safety report is still to come, so the claims are Meta's own and unverified.

Anthropic red team: small open models may soon spread across cheap IoT devices without a datacenter

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team published research arguing that small AI models could soon replicate themselves across poorly secured consumer devices such as off-brand Android TV boxes. In a firewalled eight-node test network, GLM-5.2 reached every node in four of five trials; other open-weight models failed reliably, and the exploit used (open ADB) was deliberately trivial, so the authors call it an upper bound. A 26B mixture-of-experts model ran across 15 roughly $55 TV boxes, but at only 0.39 tokens per second, and spread would be slow, with doubling times of days. The authors say no working rogue replicator exists yet but the individual steps are improving, and point to network monitoring, quarantine and better device defaults as defences.

Z.ai's GLM-Legal: a legal-domain agent model built on GLM-5.3-Flash

Z.ai released GLM-Legal, a legal-work model built by mid-training and reinforcement learning on top of the lightweight GLM-5.3-Flash. It scores 19.2% all-pass on Harvey's LAB benchmark of law-firm tasks across 24 practice areas, 12.5 points above its base model, and Z.ai says it matches frontier closed models on LegalBench and the Chinese PLawBench. Vals AI independently ran the Harvey LAB and LegalBench results; PLawBench is Z.ai's own. A low absolute all-pass rate on strict grading is the honest headline: domain tuning helps a small model, but reliable end-to-end legal work is far off. The post does not state weight availability or pricing.

AMD Helion: an open rack blueprint for agentic AI, due November

AMD introduced Helion, an Open Compute Project-based rack design for running AI agents. It comes in a CPU-only configuration with 6th-gen EPYC (up to 32,768 cores per rack) and an all-in-one configuration that adds Instinct MI350P GPU nodes, in liquid- or air-cooled versions, with a token router that sends agent model calls across frontier and open models. Racks are planned for November 2026 through partners including Dell, HPE, Lenovo, Supermicro and Cisco, and AMD intends to contribute the spec to OCP. The release gives no memory, power or performance figures, so the case for CPU-heavy agent racks rests on positioning rather than numbers.

NVIDIA opens Vera Rubin rack designs to OCP and backs a new pluggable-optics form factor with Cisco

At the OCP Global Summit NVIDIA contributed Vera Rubin rack and compute-tray designs through its MGX architecture. MGX 1.2 adds blind-mate liquid cooling, a liquid-cooled 5,000A busbar and racks up to 250 kW, and a Vera CPU rack design targets agent workloads such as tool calling. NVIDIA also open-sourced parts of its DSX OS rack-management software, and Meta is adopting Spectrum-6 102.4 Tb/s Ethernet switches for larger Vera Rubin clusters. Separately, Cisco announced the USFP pluggable-optics form factor, aimed at 400 Gb/s per lane and about 1.3x better power efficiency. Taken with AMD's Helion on the same day, both big GPU vendors are now pushing open rack specs and CPU-side racks for agents.

ReplaySSM: NVIDIA and Princeton speed up decoding for hybrid state-space models

NVIDIA and Princeton's Dao AI Lab describe ReplaySSM, a way to decode hybrid Mamba-style and attention models by storing a state checkpoint plus a buffer of recent inputs instead of rewriting the full state every step. On a B300 against a tuned vLLM baseline at batch size 256, they report 1.46–1.80x faster state-space kernels and 1.20–1.47x faster full decode steps, and 1.85–2.10x higher speculative-decoding throughput on two 120B-class models. There is no gain at batch size 1, so it helps high-concurrency serving rather than single-user latency. Numbers are the authors' own and cover Nemotron-3 and Qwen3.5 models.

Unsloth adds in-app browser use, Qwen-Image-2.1-Turbo and text-diffusion training

Unsloth's latest beta lets local or API-hosted models drive a browser tab from the chat UI, asking before sign-ins, form submissions or buy/send/delete actions and never filling passwords or card numbers. It also adds the 8-step Qwen-Image-2.1-Turbo (a 4.2 GB Q4 GGUF, claimed 2.5x faster image generation), a trainer for text-diffusion language models including LLaDA and Dream, up to 3x faster MoE GGUFs larger than VRAM, and up to 2.8x faster speculative decoding on Apple Silicon via MLX. Speedups are Unsloth's own figures. Ollama also shipped v0.40.4, bumping llama.cpp to b11521 for decision models and EmbeddingGemma 2.
